Research Area 03

Choices for Better Online Learning

COVID-19 forced schools and communities to make important decisions about how education could continue during lockdowns. This page explores the responses, decisions, and possible improvements that could make online learning more effective and accessible.

Explore Research Key Choices

Research focus

Making Better Decisions

Governments, schools and families made choices that shaped who could keep learning during lockdown and how well they were supported.

≈70% of school leaders said they explicitly prioritised wellbeing over academic learning (ERO, 2020a).

Key Areas

What Choices Could Be Made?

Choices cover access to technology, teaching approaches and support for students, teachers and families.

Access to Technology

In April 2020 the government announced a package of television, printed packs, devices and internet connections (Hipkins, 2020).

Teaching and Learning Approaches

Teachers chose between live, recorded, online and printed approaches; senior students valued varied activities and feedback (Yates & Starkey, 2021).

Support for Students and Teachers

Schools prioritised wellbeing and whānau contact; partnering with iwi and community groups worked best (Ministry of Education, 2025). Wellbeing-first teaching strengthened family relationships (Hunter et al., 2022).

Research Findings

What Decisions Were Made?

Key decisions.

Choice 1: Wellbeing before coursework

Leaders reduced academic pressure to protect wellbeing.

Evidence: Nearly three-quarters (around 70%) explicitly prioritised wellbeing over academic learning, and teachers likewise shifted from assessment to belonging and wellbeing (Ritchie, 2023).

Source: ERO, 2020a; Ritchie, 2023

Choice 2: Alternatives to online learning

Television and printed packs supported households without reliable internet.

Evidence: The package had two education TV channels (one in te reo Māori), printed packs, devices and $87.7 million in emergency funding.

Source: Hipkins, 2020; Education Gazette, 2020

Choice 3: Keeping what worked

Schools could choose which digital practices to keep.

Evidence: Two-thirds of leaders intended to keep some distance or digital learning.

Source: ERO, 2020a

Looking Forward

How Could Online Learning Be Improved?

Schools and policymakers can prepare better for the next disruption. Yates & Starkey, 2021 call for resilience plans that build digital readiness.

Short-Term Improvements

Keep regular contact with students and whānau (80% of students said teachers checked on their wellbeing; ERO, 2020b) and offer technical help. Teacher–parent collaboration also matters (Fletcher et al., 2022).

Long-Term Improvements

Build a coherent, system-wide digital plan and develop teacher and leader capability (Wenmoth, 2022) instead of relying on emergency e-learning (Trafford et al., 2021).

Evaluation

Which Choices Could Work Best?

Our evaluation: combining device access, varied formats and wellbeing-focused teaching looks most effective.

Potential Benefits

More students keep learning, and varied activities with feedback support engagement (Yates & Starkey, 2021).

Possible Limitations

Over half of 2020 device recipients had to return devices, and impact on learning could not be measured (Ministry of Education, 2022).

Fairness and Accessibility

Partnering with iwi, translating materials and prioritising low-decile schools helped reach disadvantaged families (Ministry of Education, 2025).

Critical Assessment

What Were the Trade-offs?

Each choice solved one problem and created another. Weighing them against the evidence shows which were best supported.

Wellbeing versus progress

About 70% of leaders put wellbeing before academic learning (ERO, 2020a), and wellbeing-first teaching strengthened family relationships (Hunter et al., 2022). The cost is hard to judge, because senior students reported heavy workloads and lost routine (ERO, 2020b; Yates & Starkey, 2021) while the Ministry could not measure the impact on learning (Ministry of Education, 2022). The choice looks defensible, but its academic price is unmeasured.

Universal versus targeted support

The 2020 package gave every household an option, with television, printed packs, devices and internet connections (Hipkins, 2020). Universal provision still missed some learners, and over half of device recipients had to return their devices (Ministry of Education, 2022). Partnering with iwi and community groups, translating materials and prioritising low-decile schools reached disadvantaged families better (Ministry of Education, 2025).

Emergency fix versus lasting design

Trafford et al. (2021) argue against relying on emergency e-learning, and Wenmoth, 2022 calls for a coherent, system-wide digital plan and stronger teacher and leader capability. Fletcher et al. (2022) add that teacher–parent collaboration matters. Devices that were returned after the first lockdown suggest that temporary fixes left little lasting capability.

Our recommendation

The best-supported choices are early device and connectivity provision, varied learning formats, wellbeing-first contact with whānau, and investment in teacher capability. The combination is better than any single option because devices alone missed some learners (Ministry of Education, 2022), and wellbeing contact alone could not reach families without phones or internet (Ritchie, 2023). Each element is backed by at least two sources above, but none has been tested against learning outcomes, so evaluation should be built into the next response.

Measuring Success

How Would We Know a Response Worked?

These measures are our own proposal, not findings from the research. The baselines come from the sources cited, so a future response can be compared against them.

Access

Track the share of learners with a working device and internet at home, and how many devices stay with families. Baseline: over half of 2020 device recipients had to return them (Ministry of Education, 2022).

Engagement

Track the share of teachers reporting positive engagement and the share of students able to learn from home. Baselines: 27% of teachers, 78% of primary and 58% of secondary students (ERO, 2020b).

Wellbeing and coping

Track how many students, especially in Years 11–13, say they are coping well, and add a measure of learning progress, which the Ministry could not provide in 2020 (Ministry of Education, 2022). Baseline: 26% of senior secondary students (ERO, 2020b).

Equity

Report every measure by decile, Māori and Pacific learners so gaps are visible, and judge success by whether they narrow. Baseline: 19% of decile 1–3 students shared a device, against 14% in decile 4–10 (ERO, 2020b).

Conclusion

Preparing for the Future

Prioritising wellbeing, offering alternatives and keeping effective digital practices helped learning continue, but access gaps remained. Future planning should combine early device provision, flexible formats and community partnerships.
