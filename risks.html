Research Area 02

The Risks of Online Learning

Moving education online during COVID-19 created challenges for students, teachers, and families. This page explores the potential risks and difficulties associated with the transition to online learning in New Zealand.

Explore Research Key Risks

Research focus

Challenges Beyond the Classroom

Online learning kept lessons going, but research shows risks for engagement, wellbeing, teacher workload and equal access.

27% of teachers said their students had been positively engaged in the past week of the 2020 lockdown (ERO, 2020b).

Key Areas

Major Challenges

Risks cluster around engagement and wellbeing, teaching challenges and digital inequality.

Student Engagement and Wellbeing

Teachers named students not participating (64%) and distractions at home (48%) as the main reasons for poor engagement (ERO, 2020b). Senior students cited low motivation and lost routine (Yates & Starkey, 2021).

Teaching and Technology Challenges

Only 37% of teachers could monitor student progress, and workload pressure kept growing (ERO, 2020b; ERO, 2021b).

Digital Inequality

In decile 1–3 schools 19% of students shared a device, versus 14% in decile 4–10 schools (ERO, 2020b). Existing inequities disproportionately affected Pacific students (Thomsen et al., 2021).

Research Findings

What Did the Research Reveal?

Key findings.

Finding 1: Senior students struggled most

Year 11–13 students faced heavier workloads and found timely help harder to get.

Evidence: Only 26% of senior secondary students said they were coping well with schoolwork.

Source: ERO, 2020b

Finding 2: Teacher workload kept rising

Teachers adapted quickly, but pressure grew rather than eased.

Evidence: By mid-2021 only 32% of teachers and 19% of principals said their workload was manageable, down from 42% and 26% in 2020.

Source: ERO, 2021b

Finding 3: Access gaps were not fully closed

Some learners still lacked suitable access, and devices were recalled.

Evidence: Over half of 2020 device recipients had to return devices to school after the first lockdown.

Source: Ministry of Education, 2022

Who Was Affected?

Understanding the Impact

Risks fell hardest on senior students, lower-decile schools and households with limited technology or support (Royal Commission, 2024).

Students and Families

Students lost routine and social contact. After lockdown only 58% felt safe from COVID-19, down from 85% during it (ERO, 2021a).

Teachers and Schools

Teachers learned tools quickly and monitored progress remotely; lecturers struggled to offer hands-on practice, and with most students’ cameras off it was hard to read their understanding (Godber & Atkins, 2021; Joseph & Trinick, 2021).

Confidence Rating

How Confident Are We in Each Risk?

Not every finding carries the same weight. We sort the risks by how strongly the evidence supports them.

Well supported

Senior students struggled: only 26% said they were coping well (ERO, 2020b), and a separate study found low motivation and lost routine (Yates & Starkey, 2021). Teacher workload kept rising (ERO, 2021b). Access gaps persisted, because over half of device recipients had to return devices (Ministry of Education, 2022; Wenmoth, 2022). Each rests on more than one independent source or on a large official evaluation.

Plausible but less certain

We argue that inequality compounded the risks through home support as well as devices, but this is our reading of a modest device gap (ERO, 2020b) and the Royal Commission (Royal Commission, 2024). The Pacific-student finding comes from nine Pacific academics reflecting on their own teaching (Thomsen et al., 2021), and the difficulty of reading students on camera comes from two reflective accounts (Godber & Atkins, 2021; Joseph & Trinick, 2021).

Still unknown

Long-term learning loss is unproven, because ERO’s survey was taken during lockdown and the Ministry could not measure the impact on learning (Ministry of Education, 2022). Effects on particular groups, such as Pacific learners, rest mainly on small reflective studies (Thomsen et al., 2021) rather than large samples.

Why the grading matters

Policy should act now on the well-supported risks, test the plausible ones before building programmes around them, and measure the unknown ones in the next disruption rather than assume them.

Critical Evaluation

How Reliable Is the Evidence?

The risks are better documented than the opportunities, but the sources measure different things at different times.

Risks built up over time

Teacher workload that was manageable for 42% of teachers in 2020 was manageable for only 32% by mid-2021 (ERO, 2021b), and only 58% of students felt safe from COVID-19 after lockdown, down from 85% during it (ERO, 2021a). The pressure did not end when lockdown did, so lockdown-era surveys understate the cost.

Inequality compounded every risk

In decile 1–3 schools 19% of students shared a device, against 14% in decile 4–10 schools (ERO, 2020b), and existing inequities disproportionately affected Pacific students (Thomsen et al., 2021). The device gap is modest, so we read it as one part of a wider disadvantage that also includes home support. The Royal Commission reports the same pattern, though it is a lessons-learned review rather than a new study (Royal Commission, 2024).

Gaps in the evidence

ERO’s survey was taken during lockdown, so it cannot show long-term effects. The Ministry’s evaluation could not measure the impact on learning (Ministry of Education, 2022), and the university findings (Godber & Atkins, 2021; Joseph & Trinick, 2021) are reflective accounts by two lecturers each, with no student data. We therefore treat claims about lasting learning loss as unproven rather than false.

Where sources agree

Despite different methods and samples, independent sources point the same way. Senior students struggled in both ERO’s national survey and the separate study by Yates & Starkey, 2021. Lecturers in two separate reflective accounts found it hard to read students who kept their cameras off (Godber & Atkins, 2021; Joseph & Trinick, 2021). Access gaps appear in the Ministry’s evaluation and in Wenmoth, 2022. Where studies converge, we treat the risk as well supported.

Conclusion

What Can We Learn From These Risks?

The main risks were disengagement, uneven wellbeing, rising workload and unequal access. Future plans should include earlier device provision, support for senior students and manageable workloads.
