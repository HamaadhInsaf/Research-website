Research Area 01

Opportunities of Online Learning

The shift to online learning during COVID-19 changed how students learned and how teachers delivered education. This page explores the potential benefits and opportunities that emerged during this period.

Explore Research Key Areas

Research focus

Learning Beyond the Classroom

Online learning gave students and teachers new ways to learn, teach and stay connected, but benefits depended on access.

83% of students knew how to contact their teacher for help during the 2020 lockdown (ERO, 2020b).

Key Areas

Potential Opportunities

Opportunities fall into flexibility, digital skills and new teaching methods.

Flexible Learning

Students could keep learning from home, and senior students most wanted to keep the flexibility it offered (Yates & Starkey, 2021). University staff reported flexibility for themselves and increased learner autonomy for students (Erlam et al., 2021).

Digital Skills and Technology

Students and teachers gained experience with video-conferencing and online platforms. ERO calls increased digital use the most lasting change to how schools teach (ERO, 2021b).

New Teaching Methods

Teachers could mix recorded lessons, online discussion and live classes, and schools could explore blended learning (Wenmoth, 2022).

Research Findings

What Did the Research Find?

Key findings.

Finding 1: Learners stayed connected

Most students knew how to reach their teachers, who also checked in on them.

Evidence: 83% knew how to contact their teacher, and 80% said a teacher had checked on their wellbeing.

Source: ERO, 2020b

Finding 2: Stronger family links

Online contact gave schools extra ways to reach students and families.

Evidence: A third of school leaders wanted to build on improved communication with whānau (preliminary interview findings), and teachers reported stronger, more open relationships with families (Ritchie, 2023).

Source: ERO, 2020a; Ritchie, 2023

Finding 3: Government support widened access

The Ministry tried to connect households and supply devices.

Evidence: It aimed to connect 80,000 learners in 45,000 households and supplied devices to 36,000 learners.

Source: Ministry of Education, 2022

Reflection

How Significant Were These Opportunities?

These opportunities were real but unequal. Many learners and teachers also lacked digital skills, and students with fewer devices or less home support gained less (Wenmoth, 2022; Rood & Barbour, 2024).

Impact on Students

Students gained flexibility, but senior students faced heavy workloads and slow teacher support (ERO, 2020b).

Impact on Teachers

Teachers built digital skills and tried blended approaches, though workload also rose (Wenmoth, 2022). Breakout rooms helped build students’ confidence (Joseph & Trinick, 2021).

Critical Evaluation

How Strong Is the Evidence?

The opportunities were real, but the evidence behind them is uneven. Comparing sources shows where we can be confident and where we should be cautious.

Flexibility cuts both ways

Senior students most wanted to keep the flexibility of learning from home (Yates & Starkey, 2021). Yet in the same national survey only 26% of senior secondary students said they were coping well, and only 27% of teachers reported positive engagement (ERO, 2020b). Flexibility seems to have helped students who could direct their own learning and to have cost those who could not.

Whose opportunity was it?

Access alone was not enough, because many learners and teachers lacked digital skills, and students with fewer devices or less home support gained less (Wenmoth, 2022; Rood & Barbour, 2024). Over half of 2020 device recipients later had to return their devices (Ministry of Education, 2022). The opportunities were therefore conditional on resources that were unevenly spread.

Limits of the evidence

Most figures are self-reported by students and teachers, which shows perceptions rather than learning outcomes. The finding that a third of leaders wanted to build on whānau communication comes from preliminary interviews (ERO, 2020a), and Ritchie (2023) rests on open survey answers from 51 self-selected teachers. The university evidence is also narrow: Erlam et al. (2021) surveyed 67 staff at one university, with a response rate of about 13.5%. These sources show what was possible, not how common it was.

Where the evidence is strongest

Contact between schools and families is the best-supported opportunity. It appears in a national survey, where 83% of students knew how to contact a teacher and 80% said a teacher had checked on their wellbeing (ERO, 2020b), in teachers’ accounts (Ritchie, 2023) and in leaders’ plans (ERO, 2020a). The digital-skills gain is weaker: ERO describes increased digital use as the lasting change (ERO, 2021b), which is use rather than measured skill.

Conclusion

Lessons for the Future

New Zealand can build on these gains by keeping the digital practices that worked while making sure every learner has devices, connectivity and support.
